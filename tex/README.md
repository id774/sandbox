# TeX

## Overview

TeX is a typesetting system that is also, at its core, a full macro programming language. It gives authors and document designers fine-grained control over the placement of text, symbols, and mathematical notation on a page, and it is best known for the quality of the output it produces for material that is difficult to typeset well by conventional means, particularly mathematics. Rather than being used directly by most end users, TeX more commonly serves as the underlying engine for higher-level formats and macro packages that provide friendlier, document-oriented commands.

## History

TeX was designed and originally implemented by Donald Knuth, a computer scientist who created it after becoming dissatisfied with the typeset quality of the galley proofs he received for a new edition of his book series The Art of Computer Programming. Frustrated that available phototypesetting technology could not produce the results he wanted, Knuth set out to write his own typesetting program, releasing the first version of TeX in 1978. He continued to revise the system substantially in the years that followed, and by the late 1980s TeX had been rewritten and stabilized into the version, generally known as TeX3, that remains the basis for the system today. From that point, Knuth adopted an unusual approach to versioning and maintenance: rather than continuing to add features, he declared the system essentially frozen, with new version numbers formed by appending an additional digit so that they converge toward the mathematical constant pi, and he encouraged further innovation to happen in separate programs built on top of TeX rather than in TeX itself. To encourage bug reports, Knuth offers a small monetary reward to the first person who reports each newly found error, with the reward for programming bugs having increased over the years while remaining a token amount by design, since so few bugs are left to be found.

## Architecture and characteristics

TeX operates as a text-processing system in which a document is written using commands and user- or package-defined macros; when TeX processes the document, it expands these macros and commands, ultimately reducing the input to a set of low-level typesetting instructions. Because its macro language allows arbitrary manipulation of these commands, TeX's macro-expansion mechanism is powerful enough to be used as a general-purpose programming language in its own right, independent of its typesetting functions. Once macros are expanded, TeX lays out the resulting material using a model based on boxes and glue: boxes are rigid rectangular units holding characters or other content, while glue represents the flexible space between boxes that can stretch or shrink so that lines and pages can be broken and justified in a visually balanced way. This layout model, combined with algorithms for line breaking and page breaking, underlies TeX's particular strength in typesetting mathematical formulas, where symbols, fractions, exponents, and other notation must be sized and positioned precisely relative to one another. TeX was developed together with a companion program called Metafont, which is used to describe and generate the outlines of typefaces as parameterized shapes rather than fixed glyphs, allowing fonts to be produced in variations by adjusting their defining parameters.

## Ecosystem and use

Because working directly in TeX's low-level commands is cumbersome for everyday document preparation, most users interact with TeX indirectly through higher-level macro packages. The most widely used of these is LaTeX, which layers document-structuring commands, such as those for sections, references, and bibliographies, on top of TeX's typesetting engine, and other formats and macro packages built on the same underlying engine also exist. Owing to its precision with mathematical and technical notation, TeX and the formats built upon it are used extensively in academic and scientific publishing, particularly in fields such as mathematics, computer science, and physics, where documents frequently combine prose with complex mathematical expressions.
